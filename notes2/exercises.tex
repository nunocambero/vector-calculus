\section*{Chapter 2.1}
\subsection*{Exercise 11}
\textbf{Given the set of points \(\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}\), and the function \(f(m, b)\) defined as:}
\[f(m, b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - mx_i - b)^2,\]

\textbf{Minimize \(f(m, b)\) using the method of least squares to find the best fit line \(y = mx + b\) for the given data points.}
\vskip 0.5cm
We first compute the partial derivatives of \(f(m, b)\) with respect to \(m\) and \(b\), and set them to zero to find the critical points.:
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial m} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i (y_i - mx_i - b) = 0,\]
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial b} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - mx_i - b) = 0.\]

Solving the second equation for \(b\) gives:
\[b = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i - m \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i = \bar{y} - m \bar{x},\]

where \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\) are the means of the \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) values, respectively. Substituting this expression for \(b\) back into the first equation, we can solve for \(m\):
\[-2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i (y_i - mx_i - (\bar{y} - m \bar{x})) = 0.\]

Expanding and simplifying this equation leads to:
\[\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i - m \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2 - \bar{y} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i + m \bar{x} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i = 0.\]

Then we can rearrange this to find \(m\):
\[m = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i - \bar{y} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2 - \bar{x} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}.\]

\pagebreak

Otherwise, we can express \(y_i\) in terms of the best fit line:
\[y_i = m x_i + b,\]

and form the matrix equation:
\[ A \begin{bmatrix} m \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{y},\]

where \(A\) is the matrix of \(x_i\) values and a column of ones, and \(\mathbf{y}\) is the vector of \(y_i\) values. 

\begin{multicols}{2}
This is an inconsistent system, although there exists a solution:
\[\begin{bmatrix} \hat{m} \\ \hat{b} \end{bmatrix},\]

such that:
\[\| Y - A\hat{X} \|^2 \leq \| Y - AX \|^2 \quad \text{for all } X,\]

where \(\hat{Y} \in \text{Col}(A)\) and \(Y - \hat{Y} \in (\text{Col}(A))^{\perp}\).

\end{multicols}

Then, \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix}\) such that 
\[a_i^T (Y - A\hat{X}) = 0 \quad \text{for } i = 1, 2.\]

The expression above can be rewritten as:
\[A^T (Y - A\hat{X}) = 0 \implies A^T Y = A^T A \hat{X} \implies \hat{X} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T Y,\]

i.e., the normal equations for least squares. Writing
\[F(X) = f(m, b) = \|Y - AX\|^2 = (Y - AX)^T (Y - AX) = Y^T Y - Y^T A X - X^T A^T Y + X^T A^T A X,\]

we can compute the gradient of \(F\) as:
\[\nabla F(X) = -2 A^T Y + 2 A^T A X.\]

Setting \(\nabla F(X) = 0\) gives us the normal equations again:
\[A^T A X = A^T Y \implies X = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T Y.\]

\subsection*{Exercise 16}
\textbf{Given the function \(h : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) differentiable around \(-1\) such that \(h(-1) = 1\) and, }
\[f(x, y) = h(xy) + 2h\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) - 4, \quad \text{for } x \neq 0,\]
\textbf{find the gradient of \(f\) at the point \((-1, 1)\). Then, obtain the first-order Taylor expansion of \(f\) around the point \((-1, 1)\).}
\vskip 0.5cm
To find the gradient of \(f\) at the point \((-1, 1)\), we first compute the partial derivatives of \(f\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\). Using the chain rule, we have:
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = y h'(xy) - \frac{2y}{x^2} \cdot h'\left(\frac{y}{x}\right),\]
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x h'(xy) + \left(\frac{2}{x}\right) \cdot h'\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).\]

Evaluating these derivatives at the point \((-1, 1)\), we get:
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(-1, 1) = 1 \cdot h'(-1) - \frac{2 \cdot 1}{(-1)^2} \cdot h'(-1) = h'(-1) - 2h'(-1) = -h'(-1),\]
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(-1, 1) = -1 \cdot h'(-1) + \left(\frac{2}{-1}\right) \cdot h'(-1) = -h'(-1) - 2h'(-1) = -3h'(-1).\]

Since \(h'(-1) = 1\), the gradient of \(f\) at the point \((-1, 1)\) is:
\[\nabla f(-1, 1) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}.\]

The first-order Taylor expansion of \(f\) around the point \((-1, 1)\) is given by:
\[\mathcal{P}_{1, (-1, 1)}(x, y) = f(-1, 1) + \nabla f(-1, 1) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} x + 1 \\ y - 1 \end{pmatrix}.\]

Substituting the values, we have:
\[\mathcal{P}_{1, (-1, 1)}(x, y) = -1 - h'(-1)(x + 1) - 3h'(-1)(y - 1) = -1 - (x + 1) - 3(y - 1)\]

\section*{Chapter 2.2}
\subsection*{Exercise 1}
\textbf{Given \(f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) differentiable, with \(f'(x) \neq 0, \forall x \in \mathbb{R}\), prove it is one-to-one.}
\vskip 0.5cm

\begin{proof}
    By contradiction. Assume \(f\) is not one-to-one. Then, there exist \(t_0, t_1 \in \mathbb{R}\) such that \(t_0 \neq t_1\) and \(f(t_0) = f(t_1)\). By definition:
    \[f'(t) = \lim_{t \to t_0} \frac{f(t) - f(t_0)}{t - t_0},\]
    computing \(f'(t_1)\) gives us:
    \[f'(t_1) = \lim_{t \to t_0} \frac{f(t_1) - f(t_0)}{t_1 - t_0} = 0.\]
    This would contradict the assumption that \(f'(x) \neq 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). Therefore, \(f\) must be one-to-one.
\end{proof}
\vskip 0.5cm
\textbf{Given \(F : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) a vector function defined as \(F(x, y) = (e^x \cos y, e^x \sin y)\), prove that \(F\) is not one-to-one, even though its Jacobian determinant is non-zero for all \((x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2\).}
\vskip 0.5cm
\begin{proof}
    Taking two points \(P_0 = \left(0, \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\) and \(P_1 = \left(0, \dfrac{9\pi}{4}\right)\), we have:
    \[F(P_0) = \left(e^0 \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right), e^0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right),\]
    \[F(P_1) = \left(e^0 \cos\left(\frac{9\pi}{4}\right), e^0 \sin\left(\frac{9\pi}{4}\right)\right) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right).\]
    Thus, \(F(P_0) = F(P_1)\) even though \(P_0 \neq P_1\), proving that \(F\) is not one-to-one. However, the Jacobian determinant of \(F\) is given by:
    \[\det J_F(x, y) = \det \begin{bmatrix} e^x \cos y & -e^x \sin y \\ e^x \sin y & e^x \cos y \end{bmatrix} = e^{2x} (\cos^2 y + \sin^2 y) = e^{2x} > 0 \quad \text{for all } (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2.\]
    Applying the Inverse Function Theorem, we can conclude that \(F\) is locally invertible at every point in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), but it is not globally one-to-one.
\end{proof}
\vskip 0.5cm
\textbf{If \(H\) is the inverse of \(F\) defined above, compute \(DH\) for \(F(0, 0) = (1, 0)\).}
\vskip 0.5cm
Since det \(J_F(x, y) = e^{2x} > 0\) for all \((x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2\), \(M = \left\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : -2\pi < y < 2\pi\right\}\),
\[J_F(0, 0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \implies \text{ det } J_F(0, 0) = 1 \neq 0.\]
\[J_H(F_1, F_2) = \underbrace{J_F^{-1}(F_1, F_2) = (J_F(x, y))^{-1}}_{\text{Inverse Function Theorem}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

\subsubsection*{Exercise 2}
\textbf{Given the system of equations:}
\[\begin{cases}
u = x + xyz \\
v = y + xy  \\
w = z + 2x + 3x^2
\end{cases}\]
\textbf{solve for \(x, y, z\) in terms of \(u, v, w\), near the point \((x, y, z) = (0, 0, 0)\).}
\vskip 0.5cm
Define the function \(F : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3\) as:
\[F(x, y, z) = \begin{bmatrix} x + xyz \\ y + xy \\ z + 2x + 3x^2 \end{bmatrix}.\]
We need to find the inverse of that function (if possible).
Applying the Inverse Function Theorem, we compute the Jacobian matrix of \(F\):
\[J_F(x, y, z) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + yz & xz & xy \\ y & 1 + x & 0 \\ 2 + 6x & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.\]
Evaluating the Jacobian at the point \((0, 0, 0)\) gives us:
\[J_F(0, 0, 0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.\]
The determinant of \(J_F(0, 0, 0)\) is:
\[\det J_F(0, 0, 0) = 1 \cdot (1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0) - 0 \cdot (0 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 2) + 0 \cdot (0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 2) = 1 \neq 0.\]
Since the determinant is non-zero, the Inverse Function Theorem guarantees that \(F\) is locally invertible around the point \((0, 0, 0)\). [\dots]

\subsubsection*{Exercise 3}
\textbf{Check that \(z^3 + x(z - y) = 1\) defines \(z = f(x, y)\) as an implicit function of class \(\mathcal{C}^{\infty}\) near the point \((x, y, z) = (0, 1, 1)\).}
\vskip 0.5cm
We apply the Implicit Function Theorem to find \(z\). Define the function \(F : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}\) as:
\[F(x, y, z) = z^3 + x(z - y) - 1.\]
We need to check if \(F(0, 1, 1) = 0\) and if \(J_F(0, 1, 1)\) has a non-zero entry in the column corresponding to \(z\). Evaluating \(F\) at the point gives us:
\[F(0, 1, 1) = 1^3 + 0 \cdot (1 - 1) - 1 = 0.\]
Next, we compute the Jacobian of \(F\):
\[J_F(x, y, z) = \begin{bmatrix} z - y & -x & 3z^2 + x \end{bmatrix} \implies J_F(0, 1, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.\]
So we have \(x, y\) free variables, and \(z\) is the pivot variable. Hence, we can apply the Implicit Function Theorem to conclude that there exists a function \(z = f(x, y)\) of class \(\mathcal{C}^{\infty}\) defined in a neighborhood of \((0, 1, 1)\) and satisfying \(F(x, y, f(x, y)) = 0\) for all \((x, y)\) in that neighborhood.
\vskip 0.5cm
\textbf{Study if (0, 1) is a local maximum, minimum, or saddle point of \(f\).}
\vskip 0.5cm
Using implicit differentiation, we can compute the first and second partial derivatives of \(f\) at the point \((0, 1)\). The first partial derivatives are given by:
\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} = 3 \left[z(x, y)\right]^2 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} + (z(x, y) - y) + x \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 0,\]
\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = 3 \left[z(x, y)\right]^2 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} - x + x \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = 0.\]
Evaluating these at the point \((0, 1)\) gives us:
\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}(0, 1) = 3 \cdot 1^2 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}(0, 1) + (1 - 1) + 0 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}(0, 1) = 3 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}(0, 1) = 0 \implies \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}(0, 1) = 0,\]
\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}(0, 1) = 3 \cdot 1^2 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}(0, 1) - 0 + 0 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}(0, 1) = 3 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}(0, 1) = 0 \implies \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}(0, 1) = 0.\]
Thus, \((0, 1)\) is a critical point of \(f\). To determine its nature, we need to compute the second partial derivatives and evaluate the Hessian matrix at this point.
\[\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x^2} = 6z \cdot \left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)^2 + 3z^2 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2} + 2 \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} + x \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2} = 0,\]
\[\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial y^2} = 6z \cdot \left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right)^2 + 3z^2 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2} + x \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2} = 0,\]
\[\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x \partial y} = 6z \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} + 3z^2 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x \partial y} - 1 + x \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x \partial y} = 0.\]
\newpage
Evaluating these at the point \((0, 1)\) gives us:
\[\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x^2}(0, 1) = 0 + 3 \cdot 1^2 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2}(0, 1) + 2 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2}(0, 1) = 3 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2}(0, 1) \implies \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2}(0, 1) = 0,\]
\[\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial y^2}(0, 1) = 0 + 3 \cdot 1^2 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2}(0, 1) + 0 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2}(0, 1) = 3 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2}(0, 1) \implies \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2}(0, 1) = 0,\]
\[\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x \partial y}(0, 1) = 0 + 3 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x \partial y}(0, 1) - 1 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x \partial y}(0, 1) = 3 \cdot \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x \partial y}(0, 1) - 1 \implies \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x \partial y}(0, 1) = \frac{1}{3}.\]
The determinant of the Hessian matrix of \(f\) at the point \((0, 1)\) is given by:
\[\det H_f(0, 1) = \det \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2}(0, 1) & \dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x \partial y}(0, 1) \\ \dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x \partial y}(0, 1) & \dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2}(0, 1) \end{bmatrix} = \det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \dfrac{1}{3} \\ \dfrac{1}{3} & 0 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{1}{9} < 0,\]
which indicates that \((0, 1)\) is a saddle point of \(f\).

\subsubsection*{Exercise 4}
\textbf{Given the system of equations:}
\[\begin{cases}
xz^3 + y^2u^3 - 1 = 0 \\
2xy^3 + u^2z = 0
\end{cases}\]
\textbf{find \(z = g_1(x, y)\) and \(u = g_2(x, y)\) as implicit functions.}
\vskip 0.5cm
Apply the Implicit Function Theorem to find \(z\) and \(u\). Define the function \(F : \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) as:
\[f(x, y, z, u) = \begin{bmatrix} xz^3 + y^2u^3 - 1 \\ 2xy^3 + u^2z \end{bmatrix} = (0, 0).\]
so for our notation, we have \(N = 4\) and \(M = 2\). We compute the Jacobian matrix of \(f\) at a point \((0, 1, 0, 1)\):
\[J_f(x, y, z, u) = \begin{bmatrix} z^3 & 2yu^3 & 3xz^2 & 3y^2u^2 \\ 2y^3 & 6xy^2 & u^2 & 2uz \end{bmatrix}\]
\[J_f(0, 1, 0, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.\]
We have \(x, y\) as free variables, and \(z, u\) as pivot variables. Looking for \(z = g_1(x, y)\) and \(u = g_2(x, y)\), we define \(F : \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^4\) as:
\[F(x, y, z, u) = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ xz^3 + y^2u^3 - 1 \\ 2xy^3 + u^2z \end{bmatrix}.\]
In terms of the Implicit Function Theorem, that is the splitting of the variables. The Jacobian matrix of \(F\) is given by:
\[J_F(x, y, z, u) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & \vline & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \vline & 0 & 0 \\ \hline z^3 & 2yu^3 & \vline & 3xz^2 & 3y^2u^2 \\ 2y^3 & 6xy^2 & \vline & u^2 & 2uz \end{bmatrix}.\]
The determinant of the Jacobian matrix of \(F\) is:
\[\det J_F(x, y, z, u) = \det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ z^3 & 2yu^3 & 3xz^2 & 3y^2u^2 \\ 2y^3 & 6xy^2 & u^2 & 2uz \end{bmatrix} = \det \begin{bmatrix} 3xz^2 & 3y^2u^2 \\ u^2 & 2uz \end{bmatrix} = 6xz^3u - 3y^2u^4.\]
Then, we know the existance of an inverse function so that:
\begin{align*}
    x = F_1 & & z = g_1(F_1, F_2, F_3, F_4) = g_1(x, y, F_3, F_4) \\ 
    y = F_2 & & u = g_2(F_1, F_2, F_3, F_4) = g_2(x, y, F_3, F_4)
\end{align*}

\subsection*{Exercise 3}
\textbf{Given the function \(f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) defined as \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) on \(A = \{xy = 1\}\), find the minimum of \(f\) on \(A\).}
\vskip 0.5cm
\begin{multicols}{2}
To find the minimum of \(f\) on the set \(A\), we can use the method of Lagrange multipliers. We define the constraint function \(g(x, y) = xy - 1\). The Lagrangian function is given by:
\[\mathcal{L} = f(x, y) - \lambda g(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - \lambda (xy - 1).\]
We need to compute the gradients and set them equal to zero:
\[\nabla \mathcal{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x - \lambda y \\ 2y - \lambda x \\ -(xy - 1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.\]

\end{multicols}
This gives us the system of equations:
\[\begin{cases}
2x - \lambda y = 0 \\
2y - \lambda x = 0 \\
xy - 1 = 0
\end{cases}\]
From the first two equations, we can express \(\lambda\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\):
\[\lambda = \frac{2x}{y} = \frac{2y}{x} \implies 2x^2 = 2y^2 \implies x^2 = y^2.\]
Substituting \(y = \pm x\) into the constraint \(xy = 1\) gives us:
\[\begin{cases}
x \cdot x = 1 \implies x = 1, y = 1 \\
x \cdot (-x) = 1 \implies x = -1, y = -1
\end{cases}\]
Evaluating \(f\) at these points gives us:
\[f(1, 1) = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2,\]
\[f(-1, -1) = (-1)^2 + (-1)^2 = 2.\]
Thus, the minimum of \(f\) on the set \(A\) is \(2\), achieved at the points \((1, 1)\) and \((-1, -1)\).
